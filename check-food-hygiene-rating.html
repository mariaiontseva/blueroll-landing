How to check a food hygiene rating

Every food business in the UK is inspected by local authority Environmental Health Officers (EHOs) and given a rating from 0 to 5 under the Food Hygiene Rating Scheme (FHRS). This rating tells you how well the business handles food safety at the time of inspection. Checking a restaurant's food hygiene rating before you visit is one of the simplest ways to protect yourself and your family.

What is the Food Hygiene Rating Scheme?

The FHRS is run by the Food Standards Agency (FSA) in partnership with local authorities across England, Wales, and Northern Ireland. Scotland runs a separate Food Hygiene Information Scheme (FHIS) with pass/fail results rather than a numbered scale. The scheme covers any business that supplies food to the public, including restaurants, takeaways, cafes, pubs, hotels, supermarkets, and even mobile food vans.

Inspections are carried out by trained Environmental Health Officers who assess three key areas: how hygienically the food is handled, the condition of the building and facilities, and how well the business manages food safety. Each area is scored, and the overall rating is determined by the lowest-scoring area. This means a restaurant could have excellent food handling but still receive a lower overall rating if its building maintenance or management procedures fall short.

What do the ratings mean?

The ratings range from 0 (urgent improvement necessary) to 5 (very good). Here is a quick summary of what each level indicates:

  • 5 — Very good. The highest rating. Hygiene standards are very good and the business fully complies with food safety law.
  • 4 — Good. Hygiene standards are good, with only minor issues identified.
  • 3 — Generally satisfactory. Hygiene standards are acceptable but some improvement is needed.
  • 2 — Improvement necessary. Significant issues found. The business needs to make improvements.
  • 1 — Major improvement necessary. Serious problems with food hygiene. Significant work is needed.
  • 0 — Urgent improvement necessary. The most serious outcome. The business may pose an imminent risk to public health.

The three scoring areas

Environmental Health Officers assess three areas during an inspection. Each area is scored independently, and these scores collectively determine the overall rating:

  1. Hygienic food handling — This covers how food is prepared, cooked, reheated, cooled, and stored. Inspectors check cross-contamination controls, temperature management, and how staff handle allergens. Good practices include using separate chopping boards for raw and cooked food, storing raw meat below ready-to-eat items in the fridge, and cooking food to safe internal temperatures.
  2. Cleanliness and condition of facilities and building — Also called the structural assessment. This examines the physical state of the premises: cleanliness, layout, lighting, ventilation, hand-washing facilities, and pest control measures. Inspectors look for clean surfaces, adequate hand wash basins with hot water and soap, and evidence that pest control is managed.
  3. Management of food safety — This looks at the systems in place to ensure food safety is maintained over time. Inspectors want to see documented food safety procedures (such as HACCP-based systems like Safer Food, Better Business), staff training records, and evidence that temperature checks are being logged. This is often the area where businesses lose marks, as it requires consistent record-keeping.

Why should you check before you eat?

Food hygiene ratings give you an honest snapshot of how a business handles food safety. While a single inspection does not tell the whole story — standards can improve or decline between visits — the rating is still the best publicly available indicator of a food business's commitment to safety. A business with a rating of 5 has demonstrated that it meets all legal requirements and follows best practices at the time of inspection.

Checking ratings is especially important if you or someone in your group has food allergies. Businesses with strong food safety management (reflected in higher ratings) are more likely to handle allergens correctly and have proper procedures for preventing cross-contamination.

What if a business has a low rating?

A low rating does not necessarily mean the food will make you ill, but it does indicate that improvements are needed. The business may have issues with record-keeping, building maintenance, or food handling practices. Most low-rated businesses take steps to improve and can request a re-inspection once they have addressed the problems. If you are concerned, you can choose to eat elsewhere or contact your local authority for more details about the inspection findings.
