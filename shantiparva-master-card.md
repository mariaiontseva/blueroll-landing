2. **Hard vocabulary** not yet on the previous lists (specialist register)
3. **Reading apparatus**: real ślokas with full analysis,
   syntax of the didactic register, intertextuality with Upaniṣads / Gītā,

## III. Reading Apparatus — Real Verses with Full Analysis

Six famous ślokas you should be able to gloss live in an exam.

### 1. *matsya-nyāya* — the law of the fish (MBh XII.67.16, vulgate)

> **यथा मत्स्या जले सूत्रं मण्डलं छिद्यमानकम्।**
> **तद्वत् क्षीयन्ते लोकेऽस्मिन्नराजके परस्परम्॥**
>
> *yathā matsyā jale sūtraṃ maṇḍalaṃ chidyamānakam |*
> *tadvat kṣīyante loke 'sminn arājake parasparam ||*

(One canonical formulation; the verse is paraphrased many times.
A more frequently quoted variant runs:)

> **दण्डाभावे कुतो धर्मः अराजकं हि सीदति।**
> **यथा हि मत्स्या मत्स्येन भक्ष्यन्ते तत्र दुर्बलाः॥**

— "Where there is no rod, where is dharma? A kingless realm collapses,
just as in the law of the fish the weak are devoured by the strong."

**Apparatus:**
- *arājaka* — bahuvrīhi: "one in which there is no king."
- *matsya-nyāya* — genitive tatpuruṣa, "rule of the fish."
- *bhakṣyante* — passive present 3 pl. of √*bhakṣ*: "are devoured."
- *parasparam* — adverb, "one against another."
- Doctrinal context: justifies institution of kingship and *daṇḍa-nīti*.

### 2. *icchā-mṛtyu* — Bhīṣma's death-at-will (sense paraphrase)

> "Pierced with arrows I lie, yet I do not die, for the boon
> of *icchā-mṛtyu* was granted me by Śāntanu my father.
> I await the auspicious turn of the sun (*uttarāyaṇa*),
> and then alone shall I depart."

**Apparatus:**
- *icchā-mṛtyu* — bahuvrīhi: "death (which is) at (one's) wish."
- *uttarāyaṇa* — "northern course" — winter solstice when the sun turns
  north; the auspicious half-year for dying.
- Narrative function: explains why this huge teaching is even possible
  — Bhīṣma is a corpse-in-waiting, lecturing in extra time.

### 3. *daiva* vs. *pauruṣa* (MBh XII.139, paraphrased)

> "Some say all is *daiva* (fate); others say all is *pauruṣa* (effort).
> Neither alone explains the world. The wise know:
> effort applied within fate yields the fruit."

**Apparatus:**
- A central debate, recurring throughout Śāntiparva.
- *daiva* — n. a-stem from *deva*, "what comes from the gods, the
  predetermined."
- *pauruṣa* — n. a-stem from *puruṣa*, "what is of a man, manly effort."
- Bhīṣma's position: both real, neither sufficient alone.

### 4. *the four upāyas* (gnomic formulation)

> **साम्ना दानेन भेदेन दण्डेनैव च भारत।**
> **साधयेत् कार्यमर्थानां येन येन यथा यथा॥**
>
> *sāmnā dānena bhedena daṇḍenaiva ca bhārata |*
> *sādhayet kāryam arthānāṃ yena yena yathā yathā ||*

— "By conciliation, by gift, by dissension, and by force, O Bhārata,
let the king accomplish the aims of state, by whichever, however
(works)."

**Apparatus:**
- All four nouns in instrumental: instrument of policy.
- *sādhayet* — optative 3 sg. caus. of √*sādh*: "let him bring about."
- *yena yena yathā yathā* — distributive repetition: "by whichever
  means, in whatever way" — emphatic flexibility.

### 5. *yataḥ kṛṣṇaḥ tato dharmaḥ* — the loyalty formula

> **यतः कृष्णस्ततो धर्मो यतो धर्मस्ततो जयः।**
>
> "Where Kṛṣṇa is, there is dharma; where dharma is, there is victory."

**Apparatus:**
- *yataḥ ... tataḥ* — correlative "where ... there" (ablative-of-place
  correlative pair).
- Found in several Mahābhārata books; in Śāntiparva it grounds the
  theology of the *Nārāyaṇīya*.

### 6. *the saptāṅga* enumeration (paraphrase)

> **स्वाम्यमात्यजनदुर्गकोषदण्डसुहृत्तथा।**
> **एतत् सप्ताङ्गमित्याहू राज्यं तत्त्वार्थकोविदाः॥**

— "Sovereign, minister, populace, fortress, treasury, army, and ally —
this sevenfold (set) the knowers of essence call the *rājya* (state)."

**Apparatus:**
- A standard gnomic enumeration (memorized by every Sanskrit polity
  student in the tradition).
- *saptāṅga* — karmadhāraya/bahuvrīhi: "having seven limbs."
- *tattvārtha-kovidāḥ* — "those skilled in the meaning of the
  categories" = political theorists.
- Note that *daṇḍa* here = army, not "punishment." Same word, two
  technical senses depending on register.

## IV. Syntactic Patterns of the Didactic Register

Six structures you will see again and again.

### 4.1 Correlatives

- *yathā ... tathā* — "as ... so"
- *yatra ... tatra* — "where ... there"
- *yaḥ ... saḥ* — "who ... he"
- *yadā ... tadā* — "when ... then"
- *yāvat ... tāvat* — "as long as ... so long"
- *yāvān ... tāvān* — "as much as ... so much"

Pattern: relative clause **first**, main clause **second**. (Opposite
of English.) When translating, flip them.

### 4.2 Quotative *iti*

The closing particle *iti* marks the boundary of a quotation, thought,
or named thing. Most often: "X *iti* + verb of saying/thinking."

- *... iti uvāca* — "saying ... he said."
- *... iti me matiḥ* — "such is my view." (signature closure of
  Bhīṣma)
- *... iti śrūyate* — "thus it is heard (in tradition)."

### 4.3 The *manye / manyate* + accusative+accusative construction

*X-am Y-am manye* — "I consider X to be Y."

> *taṃ manye puruṣaṃ dhīram*
> — "I consider that man wise."

Both *taṃ* and *dhīram* are accusatives, agreeing.

### 4.4 Absolute construction: locative absolute

> *gate rājani sā gṛham agāt*
> — "the king having gone, she went home."

Subject and participle both in **locative**. Frequent in narrative.
Don't translate the locative literally — it's a "having X-ed" clause.

### 4.5 *sati saptamī* — "in the existence of X"

A subtype of locative absolute with *sati / satyām*:
> *jīvati pitari* — "while the father lives"

### 4.6 *iti uktvā / iti śrutvā* — discourse hinges

Almost every chapter of Śāntiparva has the rhythm:

> *... iti uktvā [name] tūṣṇīm āsīt.*
> "Having said this, [X] remained silent."

…followed by Yudhiṣṭhira's next question. Spot it and you can navigate
chapter boundaries without TOC.

## V. Intertextual Layer — what Śāntiparva is talking to

Many Śāntiparva passages echo or paraphrase older texts. Knowing
the conversation partners impresses examiners.

| Śāntiparva passage | Echoes / parallels |
|--------------------|---------------------|
| Sāṃkhya tattva-lists (Mokṣa-dharma) | *Kaṭha Upaniṣad* I.3.10–13 |
| *kṣetra-jña* doctrine | *Bhagavad-gītā* XIII (whole chapter) |
| three guṇas | *Bhagavad-gītā* XIV |
| daiva–pauruṣa debate | *Bhagavad-gītā* XVIII.13–16 (five factors of action) |
| Nārāyaṇīya pāñca-rātra | *Chāndogya Upaniṣad* (sat-doctrine), later Pāñcarātra Saṃhitās |
| śvetadvīpa episode | possibly responding to non-Vedic cults (debated: Christian? Manichaean? Bhāgavata?) |
| Śuka's liberation | echoes *Bṛhadāraṇyaka* IV.4 (Yājñavalkya's renunciation) |
| Sulabhā–Janaka | parallel to *Bṛhadāraṇyaka* III.6 (Gārgī–Yājñavalkya) |
| matsya-nyāya | *Manusmṛti* VII.20; *Arthaśāstra* I.4 |
| four upāyas | *Arthaśāstra* II.10 (and ubiquitous) |
| seven elements of state | *Arthaśāstra* VI.1 |

The Bhagavad-gītā in Bhīṣma-parva and the philosophical core of
Śāntiparva together form what Hopkins called the "epic Sanskrit
philosophy" — a single coherent project across two books.

## VI. Commentarial Tradition

The standard pre-modern commentary on the Mahābhārata is the
**Bhārata-bhāva-dīpa** ("Lamp on the meaning of the Bhārata") of
**Nīlakaṇṭha Caturdhara** (Maharashtra, ca. 1680). For Śāntiparva
specifically:

- Nīlakaṇṭha tends to read **Vedānta into everything** — he glosses
  Sāṃkhya verses through *advaita* whenever possible.
- He preserves variant readings; sometimes these match Critical
  Edition manuscripts but not the printed vulgate.
- He explicitly identifies many *upākhyānas* (vignettes) and their
  framings — useful if you're asked "who told this to whom?"
- On Nārāyaṇīya he leans toward **Pāñcarātra** identification.

Modern commentary worth knowing (in case asked):
- **V. S. Sukthankar** — editor of the Critical Edition (BORI, Poona),
  whose Prolegomena (vol. 1, 1933) sets out the *Pandava-centric* vs
  the *southern* recensional tendencies.
- **Hopkins, *The Great Epic of India* (1901)** — first major Western
  treatment of Śāntiparva's philosophy.
- **James Fitzgerald** — currently translating Books 11–13 into English
  for Chicago; his introduction to Book 12 (2004) is the most usable
  modern reading of Rājadharma.

## VII. Critical Edition vs Vulgate — What Got Moved

The **Poona Critical Edition** (Sukthankar et al., BORI, 1933–66)
treats roughly 30% of the vulgate Mokṣadharma as *prakṣipta*
(interpolation) — relegated to the apparatus or appendices.

Most consequentially in Śāntiparva:
- Several long sub-units of *Nārāyaṇīya* are heavily marked-up.
- Some long stretches of cosmological narration are condensed.
- The Critical Edition keeps the *Śuka-ākhyāna* but trims its
  expansions.
- The four-*upāya* and *saptāṅga* core passages survive intact —
  these are pan-recensional bedrock.

For exam purposes: assume any cited verse may exist in both editions
with slight differences in numbering. If your text is the BORI CE,
verse numbers may be lower than the Calcutta/Gorakhpur vulgate by
several.

## Closing rubric for the exam

1. **Locate the passage** (Rāja / Āpad / Mokṣa, ± named episode).
2. **Identify the genre** (narrative frame / gnomic verse / dialogue /
   hymn / enumeration).
3. **Identify compounds by type** (tat / karm / bahu / dvandva /
   avyay).
4. **Translate**, preserving correlative order in your gloss but
   restoring English word order.
5. **Place in doctrinal context** (which debate? which side?).
6. **Cite a parallel** from Upaniṣads/Gītā/Arthaśāstra if relevant.

You can do this. Good luck.
