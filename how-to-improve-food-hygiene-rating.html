How to improve your food hygiene rating

Practical, actionable advice for each of the three scoring areas. Whether you are stuck on a 3 or aiming for a perfect 5, this guide covers exactly what inspectors look for.

Understanding how ratings are decided

Your food hygiene rating is determined by an Environmental Health Officer (EHO) during an unannounced inspection. The inspector scores your business across three areas, each on a scale from 0 (best) to 25 (worst). These three scores are combined to produce your overall rating from 0 to 5. Crucially, the overall rating is typically limited by the worst-scoring area — so you need to perform well across all three, not just one or two.

The three areas are: hygienic food handling, cleanliness and condition of facilities, and management of food safety. We will go through each in detail below, with specific actions you can take to improve your score in each area.

1
Hygienic Food Handling

This area assesses how food is prepared, cooked, reheated, cooled, and stored in your kitchen. It is the most visible part of the inspection and often the one that kitchen teams find most intuitive. However, inspectors look for systematic practices, not just good intentions.

What inspectors check:

  • Cross-contamination controls: separate boards, utensils, and storage for raw and ready-to-eat foods
  • Temperature management during cooking (core temperature of 75°C for at least 2 seconds), cooling (to 8°C within 90 minutes), and reheating (to 82°C in Scotland, 75°C elsewhere)
  • Correct fridge storage: raw meat on the lowest shelf, ready-to-eat food above, everything covered, labelled, and dated
  • Proper thawing procedures — no thawing at room temperature
  • Allergen management: staff awareness, labelling, and ability to answer customer queries about allergens in each dish
  • Personal hygiene: handwashing before handling food, after handling raw food, using the toilet, or touching bins

How to improve:

  • Implement a colour-coded chopping board system (red for raw meat, blue for fish, green for salad, etc.) and enforce it rigorously
  • Log fridge and freezer temperatures twice daily — once at opening and once mid-service. Use a digital probe thermometer for cooking checks and record the results
  • Create a clear allergen matrix for your entire menu and train all staff (including front-of-house) to use it. Update it every time a recipe changes
  • Display handwashing instructions at every wash station and conduct refresher training every three months
  • Use a first-in, first-out (FIFO) stock rotation system with clear date labels on all opened or decanted products

2
Cleanliness and Condition of Facilities

Also known as the "structural" assessment, this area covers the physical state of your premises. Even if your food handling is excellent, a poorly maintained kitchen can drag your overall rating down significantly. Inspectors assess whether the building itself supports good food safety practices.

What inspectors check:

  • General cleanliness of all food areas: floors, walls, ceilings, work surfaces, equipment
  • Layout and workflow: is there a logical flow from delivery to storage to preparation to service, minimising cross-contamination risk?
  • Adequate hand wash basins — separate from food prep sinks, with hot and cold running water, liquid soap, and paper towels or air dryers
  • Ventilation and extraction systems in working order
  • Lighting: adequate lighting in all food areas for staff to see what they are doing
  • Pest control measures: proofing of doors and windows, no evidence of pests (droppings, gnaw marks), a pest control contract in place
  • Condition of equipment: no rust, cracks, or damaged surfaces that cannot be properly cleaned
  • Adequate waste disposal: lidded bins, regular collection, clean bin storage area

How to improve:

  • Create a deep cleaning schedule that covers every area and piece of equipment. Assign responsibilities by name and sign off completions
  • Fix any building maintenance issues immediately: cracked tiles, peeling paint, damaged seals on fridges, broken extractor fans. Do not wait for the inspection
  • Ensure every hand wash basin has hot water, soap, and drying facilities. Basins must not be used for food preparation or washing equipment
  • Install self-closing doors and fly screens on windows. Keep external areas around waste bins clean and tidy
  • Replace any equipment that is damaged beyond proper cleaning — cracked plastic containers, chipped crockery, pitted chopping boards
  • Keep a pest control contract and records of visits. Check for signs of pests weekly as part of your cleaning schedule
3
Management of Food Safety

This is the area where most businesses lose marks, and it is also where you can make the biggest improvements with the least physical change to your premises. Inspectors are looking for evidence that you have a system in place to manage food safety — and that you actually use it. This goes beyond having a manual on a shelf. It means documented procedures, completed records, and evidence of staff training.

What inspectors check:

  • A documented food safety management system based on HACCP principles (such as Safer Food, Better Business, or equivalent)
  • That the system is actively used: completed daily/weekly record sheets, not blank templates
  • Staff training records: induction training, food hygiene certificates, refresher training dates
  • Temperature monitoring records: fridge/freezer checks, cooking temperatures, hot-holding temperatures, delivery temperature checks
  • Cleaning schedules that are completed and signed, not just displayed
  • Supplier records and traceability: can you trace any ingredient back to its supplier?
  • Evidence that the system is reviewed and updated when menus change, new equipment is installed, or issues arise

How to improve:

  • If you do not already have one, implement a food safety management system. In England and Wales, the FSA provides free Safer Food, Better Business (SFBB) packs. Alternatively, use a digital system that makes compliance easier
  • Complete your records every day. The single biggest red flag for inspectors is a food safety manual that is clearly not being used. Blank forms, old dates, and missing signatures suggest the system exists only on paper
  • Log temperatures digitally. Paper logs are prone to "batch filling" (filling in a week's worth at once), which inspectors can spot. Digital records with timestamps are far more credible
  • Keep a training matrix showing every staff member's food hygiene training level and renewal dates. Ensure at least one person holds a Level 3 Food Safety certificate
  • Review your system quarterly and update it whenever you change your menu, suppliers, or procedures
  • Maintain a corrective actions log: when something goes wrong (a fridge breaks down, a supplier delivers food at the wrong temperature), document what happened and what you did about it
The management gap

Many businesses achieve strong scores in food handling and building condition but fall down on management. The reason is simple: management requires ongoing documentation. Daily temperature logs, completed cleaning schedules, training records, and supplier checks all need to be maintained consistently. This is where digital tools make the biggest difference — they make record-keeping fast, automatic, and verifiable.

The re-inspection process

If your rating is lower than you would like, you have the right to request a re-inspection. In England, you can apply for a re-visit once you have made the necessary improvements — there is usually a fee (around £150-200) and a waiting period. In Wales and Northern Ireland, re-inspections are free. Before requesting a re-inspection, make sure you have genuinely addressed all the issues raised in the inspection report. Requesting a re-inspection before you are ready will not help and may result in the same or a lower score.

You also have the right to appeal your rating if you believe the assessment was unfair, and to submit a "right to reply" which appears alongside your rating on the FSA website.

Timeline for improvement

Structural improvements (fixing building issues, installing new equipment) take time and investment. However, management improvements can begin immediately. Start logging temperatures today, complete your cleaning schedule, update your HACCP manual, and file your training records. These changes alone can often lift a business from a 3 to a 4, or from a 4 to a 5.

What to do before your next inspection

  1. Review your last inspection report — address every issue mentioned, no matter how minor it seemed
  2. Complete your food safety records — fill in every daily/weekly log for at least the past 3 months
  3. Walk your premises — look at your kitchen through an inspector's eyes. Check for cleanliness, maintenance issues, and signage
  4. Test your team — ask staff about handwashing procedures, allergens in popular dishes, and what to do if a fridge breaks down
  5. Gather your documents — ensure all certificates, training records, pest control reports, and supplier lists are accessible
  6. Check your equipment — verify that thermometers are calibrated, fridges are at the correct temperature, and all equipment is clean and in good repair

Common mistakes that cost marks

  • Open food in the fridge without date labels
  • No soap or paper towels at hand wash basins
  • Using food prep sinks for handwashing
  • Raw meat stored above ready-to-eat food
  • Blank or incomplete food safety records
  • Staff unable to explain allergen procedures
  • Broken or missing thermometers
  • Dirty extraction fans or ceiling vents
  • No evidence of pest control
  • Out-of-date food safety certificates

Each of these is straightforward to fix. The key is not to wait until an inspection is imminent — good food safety practices should be part of your daily routine.
