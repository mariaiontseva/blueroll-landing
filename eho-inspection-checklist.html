EHO inspection: what to expect and how to be ready

What happens when the environmental health officer walks in, how often they come, the three scores behind your food hygiene rating, a checklist to work through, and what to do after the visit.

Try Blueroll free

What happens on an EHO visit

The officer works to the Food Law Code of Practice, which local councils must follow.

They arrive, usually unannounced

There is no notice unless the check needs it. The officer has a legal right to enter at all reasonable hours and will show ID if you ask.

They watch how food is handled

Preparation, cooking, reheating, cooling, storage, temperature control and keeping raw and ready-to-eat food apart. They may take samples and photographs.

They look at the building

Cleanliness, layout, the condition of the structure, lighting, ventilation, handwashing and other facilities, and pest control.

They check how you manage food safety

Your Safer Food, Better Business pack or HACCP plan, the records behind it, training, your track record, whether you acted on last time's advice and how well you and your team understand the risks.

Your team is part of it

Confidence in management covers whether staff are supervised, instructed and trained, so whoever is on shift should know where the records are and explain handwashing, fridge temperatures and allergen questions.

You get your rating in writing

At the end of the visit or within 14 days, weekends and bank holidays included, with the score for each area and, below a 5, what you must do to comply.

How often EHO inspections happen

In England the gap between inspections depends on your risk category, from A to E. The Food Law Code of Practice sets the minimum for each.

Your risk score adds up what you do (the type of food and how it is handled, how many people you serve, and vulnerable groups such as children under 5 or people over 65) and your three scores at the last inspection. Better scores lower the total, so a good inspection can mean fewer visits.

New businesses

Register with your council at least 28 days before you start trading. If you prepare open high-risk food for 20 or more meals a day, as most restaurants do, the first inspection should come within 28 days of registration, or of the council finding out you are trading. Lower-hazard businesses can wait up to three months. A new owner taking over an existing business counts as new.

When they come sooner

The council can bring an inspection forward when information suggests something has changed, such as a complaint. Businesses in a recognised assurance scheme may be inspected less often. Wales and Northern Ireland have their own codes of practice, and Scotland has its own scheme (see Scotland below).

Confidence in management has no 15: it goes from 10 straight to 20, so weak management and records alone can mean a 1, and a 25 or 30 in any area means a 0.

Confidence in management is the officer's judgement on whether you will keep standards up after they leave. The Code lists what shapes it: your track record and complaint history, whether you acted on earlier advice, the attitude of management, food safety knowledge including HACCP, and procedures that suit the size and type of your business.

EHO inspection checklist

The checks follow the three scores. Tick what is true in your kitchen today, or print the list and tick it by hand. Nothing you tick leaves this page.

Food handling

Fridges at 5°C or below. By law chilled food must be at 8°C or below; setting fridges to 5°C gives you a margin. Probe one and compare it with today's log.
Freezers at around -18°C, with nothing thawed and refrozen.
Raw meat, fish and poultry kept apart from ready-to-eat food: a separate fridge, or raw food stored below.
Separate chopping boards and utensils for raw food, and staff actually use them. Colour coding helps.
Cooking checked with a clean probe in the centre. Standard advice is 70°C for 2 minutes, or 75°C for 30 seconds.
Hot food held at 63°C or above. Below 63°C for no more than 2 hours, then cooled to 8°C or below or thrown away.
Reheated food steaming hot all the way through, and reheated only once.
Hot food cooled quickly before it goes into the fridge.
Food covered, labelled and in date, nothing past its use-by date, older stock used first.
Defrosting in the fridge, not on the counter.
Hands washed with soap and warm water between tasks and after handling raw food.
Clean workwear, hair tied back, cuts covered, and staff know to report illness before a shift.
Allergen information for every dish on today's menu, and staff know where to find it.

Building and cleaning

A handwash basin used only for handwashing, with hot water, soap and paper towels, and nothing stacked in it.
Work surfaces clean and in good repair: no chips, cracks or peeling paint.
Floors, walls and ceilings clean: no grease build-up, damaged tiles or mould.
Equipment clean and working: fridges, ovens, fryers, dishwasher and extraction filters.
Good lighting and working ventilation in every food area.
No signs of pests: droppings, gnaw marks or dead insects.
Pest proofing intact: gaps sealed around pipes and doors, fly screens in place.
Bins with lids, emptied regularly, and a clean outside bin area.
Cleaning chemicals stored away from food and clearly labelled.
Separate cloths and sponges for raw food areas, or cleaned and disinfected between uses.
A working probe thermometer and probe wipes, checked for accuracy.
Chopping boards in good condition: no deep cuts or warping.

Management and records

A food safety management system written for your menu: Safer Food, Better Business or your own HACCP plan, updated when the menu changes.
Opening and closing checks done every day, filled in on the day, with no gaps.
Temperature records up to date: fridges, freezers, cooking and hot holding.
A cleaning schedule in use: what gets cleaned, how often, by whom, signed off.
Problems written down with what you did about them: corrective actions for anything out of range.
Training records on site: food hygiene certificates and induction records for new starters.
Supplier list and delivery checks, with invoices kept so you can trace food back.
Pest control contract and visit reports, with any actions taken.
Allergen records kept up to date for every dish and every menu change.
Records reviewed by a manager regularly. The SFBB pack has a 4-weekly review for this.
Every point from your last inspection letter fixed, with evidence you can show.
Staff can answer the basics: handwashing, fridge temperatures, allergens, and what to do when something goes wrong.
Your business registered with the council, with current details.

How to run a mock EHO inspection

A mock inspection is you, or someone from outside the kitchen, walking through the business the way the officer would, before the officer does. Do it on an ordinary busy day, not straight after a deep clean.

  1. Pick an ordinary service and don't warn anyone. Real inspections usually come without notice, so tidying up first defeats the point.
  2. Walk the kitchen with the checklist above. Handwash basin, fridges, storage, prep areas, bins and the back door. Probe a fridge and compare it with today's log.
  3. Watch food being handled for ten minutes. Handwashing between tasks, separate boards for raw meat, labels and dates on everything that has been opened.
  4. Ask whoever is on shift three questions. What should this fridge read? What do you do if a customer asks about allergens? What happens if a delivery arrives too warm?
  5. Pull the last four weeks of records. Look for gaps, pages filled in afterwards in the same pen, and problems with no corrective action written down.
  6. Score yourself honestly in the three areas. Use the table above to see your likely rating, then fix the weakest area first, because it sets the ceiling.

After an EHO inspection

What arrives, when your rating goes public, and what you can do if you disagree with it.

  1. 14 days

    The letter

    Your rating in writing, on the day or within 14 days.

  2. 3to5 weeks

    The rating goes online

    A 5 is published as soon as your council uploads it. Ratings of 0 to 4 go online 3 to 5 weeks after the inspection, to leave time for an appeal.

  3. 21 days

    Appeal

    If the rating does not reflect what the officer found on the day, appeal in writing within 21 days of being told it. The council's lead officer for food, not your inspector, decides within 21 days.

  4. 3 months

    Re-rating

    Accept the rating, make the improvements and ask for a new unannounced visit. It should happen within three months; when the three months start depends on your council.

What the letter includes

  • your rating and the score for each of the three areas
  • why you were rated that way and, below a 5, what you must do in each area to comply with the law
  • when the rating will be published and when you will get your sticker
  • how to appeal, the deadline, and contact details for the inspecting officer
  • whether your council charges for a re-rating visit, and how much

The officer must make clear which points are legal requirements and which are good-practice recommendations. If something is unclear, call the inspecting officer. Talking it through may settle it, but it does not stop the clock on the appeal deadline.

Right to reply

If you have fixed things since, or there were unusual circumstances on the day, you can write a right to reply any time before your next inspection. It is published next to your rating and does not change it.

Publishing and the sticker

You can ask the council to publish a new rating before the appeal period ends. In Wales and Northern Ireland you must display your sticker at or near each customer entrance; in England displaying it is voluntary.

If there are serious problems

The officer can serve a Hygiene Improvement Notice, or a Hygiene Emergency Prohibition Notice that stops you using certain equipment or processes. Either way, they will come back to check you have done what the notice requires.

Asking for a re-rating visit

Write to your council (email is fine) with what you have fixed since the inspection and your evidence, for example photos, invoices, training certificates and your records since the visit. The council can refuse a request without enough evidence. At the visit the officer looks at standards generally, not just the points you fixed, and the rating can go up, down or stay the same.

Some councils in England charge for a re-rating visit, and every council in Wales and Northern Ireland does. See what councils charge and get a free request letter.

Scotland

Scotland runs the Food Hygiene Information Scheme instead. The result is Pass or Improvement Required. You can appeal within 14 days and add a right to reply, and with Improvement Required you can ask for one revisit between routine inspections. Revisits are free at the moment.

Questions owners ask

Are EHO inspections announced?

Usually not. The Food Law Code of Practice says inspections are carried out without prior notice unless notice is needed for the check to work, so plan to be ready on any day.

Can I refuse an EHO inspection?

No. Under section 32 of the Food Safety Act 1990 an authorised officer can enter food premises at all reasonable hours. Intentionally obstructing them, or refusing without reasonable cause the help or information they need, is an offence under section 33. You can ask to see their ID.

How often does the EHO inspect a restaurant?

In England it depends on your risk category: at least every 6 months for category A, then every 12, 18, 24 and 36 months for categories B to E. A new business preparing open high-risk food for 20 or more meals a day should be inspected within 28 days of registering.

What documents should I have ready for an EHO inspection?

Your food safety management system (Safer Food, Better Business or a HACCP plan), daily opening and closing checks, temperature records, your cleaning schedule, corrective actions, staff training records, supplier details, pest control reports and allergen information for every dish.

What happens after an EHO inspection?

You get your rating in writing on the day or within 14 days, with the score for each area and what to fix. A 5 goes online as soon as the council uploads it. Ratings of 0 to 4 are published 3 to 5 weeks after the inspection, so you have time to appeal.

How do I appeal my food hygiene rating?

In England, Wales and Northern Ireland, write to your council's lead officer for food within 21 days of being told your rating. The officer who inspected you cannot decide the appeal, and you should get the decision within 21 days. In Scotland the deadline is 14 days.

How much does a food hygiene re-rating cost?

It depends where you are. Some councils in England charge and every council in Wales and Northern Ireland does. A revisit in Scotland is free at the moment. Our re-rating guide lists fees council by council.

What is a mock EHO inspection?

A practice inspection you run yourself, or ask someone from outside the kitchen to run, using the same three areas the officer scores. It shows you what to fix before the real visit. Our free self-audits walk you through one.
