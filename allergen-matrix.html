What is an allergen matrix?

An allergen matrix is a table with every dish down the side and the 14 allergens across the top. A tick shows which allergens each dish contains. Front of house uses it to answer “is there sesame in this?” from writing instead of memory, and the kitchen uses it when planning recipes. The FSA's own checklist suggests asking kitchen staff to use an allergy matrix when they plan recipes.

It is also one of the ways the FSA suggests putting allergen information in writing for customers: on the main menu, in an allergen booklet on the counter, or as a matrix displayed on a wall where customers can see it. The FSA publishes its own example matrix. This template uses the same layout and adds what a busy kitchen needs: a dated sign-off, a notes column for which cereal or nut, a may-contain mark and a change log.

Why the FSA expects your allergen information to be accurate

If you sell food that is not prepacked, such as meals in a restaurant or food from a takeaway, you must supply allergen information for every item that contains any of the 14 allergens. You can give it in writing, or verbally with a clearly visible written notice that tells customers how to get it. The FSA's advice is that written information, supported by a conversation, works best for customers.

Whatever format you choose, the information has to be right. The FSA puts it plainly: accurate allergen information “is a legal requirement”. It also expects you to know what is in your food, recorded in writing: on product specification sheets, on ingredient labels, and in your recipes, kept up to date when recipes change.

Local authorities enforce the allergen rules. If you do not act on their advice they can serve an improvement notice, and not meeting it leads to a penalty. In some cases businesses face prosecution. This FSA guidance applies to England, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The 14 allergens on the template

These are the allergens UK food law requires you to declare, in the FSA's wording and order. The rule also applies to additives, processing aids and any other substances present in the final dish. For more on each one, see our guide to the 14 allergens.

  • Celery
  • Cereals containing glutensuch as wheat, rye, barley and oats
  • Crustaceanssuch as prawns, crabs and lobsters
  • Eggs
  • Fish
  • Lupin
  • Milk
  • Molluscssuch as mussels and oysters
  • Mustard
  • Peanuts
  • Sesame
  • Soybeans
  • Sulphur dioxide and sulphitesif the sulphur dioxide and sulphites are at a concentration of more than ten parts per million
  • Tree nutssuch as almonds, hazelnuts, walnuts, brazil nuts, cashews, pecans, pistachios and macadamia nuts

Customers can be allergic to foods outside the 14 too. The FSA advises keeping full ingredient records, such as the food labels, so staff can check when asked instead of guessing.

How to fill in your allergen matrix

  1. List everything you serve. Every dish and drink, plus sauces, dressings, garnishes, toppings and sides. Allergens often hide in the parts nobody thinks of as a dish.
  2. Work from labels and recipes, not memory. Check each ingredient against its label or specification sheet. Bought-in stocks, sauces and bread count as ingredients too.
  3. Tick each allergen the dish contains. For cereals containing gluten and tree nuts, write which one in the Notes column. The FSA asks businesses to be able to name the specific cereal or nut.
  4. Use M for may contain, sparingly. Mark it only where cross-contact is real and you cannot remove it. The FSA's example: chips cooked in oil already used for battered fish cannot be served as gluten free. Do not use the matrix to make “free from” claims; the FSA treats those as a guarantee that needs strict controls.
  5. Date it, sign it and put it where people can use it. One copy at the pass, one front of house. Make sure every member of staff knows where it is.

The filled-in example on sheet 2 shows all of this on eight cafe dishes, six of them taken from the FSA's own examples.

Keeping it up to date: recipe changes and supplier swaps

A matrix is only right on the day it was checked. The FSA lists the ways to keep allergen information accurate, and most of them are about change:

  • keep records of your ingredients, and regularly check whether ingredients in products have changed;
  • keep a record of recipes, so a dish is made the same way each time;
  • keep last-minute substitutions to a minimum, and when one happens, check the ingredients and update the information;
  • use approved suppliers, and ask them to tell you about any allergen changes;
  • check regularly that the allergen information is still accurate.

In practice that means re-checking a row the same day something changes: a chef tweaks a recipe, a special goes on, a supplier swaps a brand or sends a substitute, or a product is reformulated. Read the label on the delivery, not last month's. Then write the change in the log on sheet 3, re-date and sign the matrix, and replace the copies at the pass and front of house. The FSA's manager checklist also asks whether you have clear procedures and dedicated staff for updating written allergen information, so decide who that is.

How the matrix relates to Natasha's Law

Natasha's Law covers prepacked for direct sale (PPDS) food: food packed on the same premises it is sold from, before the customer orders it, such as sandwiches, salads and pies made and packed on site. Since 1 October 2021, PPDS food needs a label with the name of the food and a full ingredients list, with the 14 allergens emphasised.

Food made to order is not PPDS, but you still have to give accurate allergen information for it. A matrix does not replace a PPDS label, which needs the full ingredients list. It is a quick way to check that your labels, your menu and your staff all say the same thing about allergens. More in our guide to Natasha's Law in 2026.

Allergen matrix questions

Is there an official FSA allergen matrix template?

The FSA publishes an example allergen matrix in its best practice guidance for non-prepacked food, and points to an editable version on its allergen icons and posters page. Both are free. This template follows the same layout and adds a dated sign-off, a notes column, a may-contain mark and a change log.

Do I have to use an allergen matrix?

No single format is required. For food that is not prepacked, you must give allergen information for every item that contains any of the 14 allergens, and it must be accurate. You can give it in writing, or verbally with a clearly visible written notice. The FSA's advice is written information supported by a conversation, and a matrix is one of the written formats it names.

How often should I update my allergen matrix?

Every time something changes that could move a tick: a recipe, an ingredient, a supplier or brand, or a substitution. The FSA also advises checking regularly that your allergen information is still accurate, so date and sign every review.

Can I fill it in on a computer?

Yes. Open the Excel file in Excel or Google Sheets, pick a tick or M from the drop-down in each allergen column, and the last column writes a Contains line you can copy onto your menu. Print it on A4 landscape when you are done.
