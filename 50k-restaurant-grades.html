Four cities. Four completely different systems.

Every major city grades restaurants differently. NYC uses letter grades that hang in the window. Chicago just says pass or fail. San Francisco gives a numeric score. London rates you 0 to 5. We analysed them all.

Chicago had the largest share labelled “Fail” in this snapshot. That should not be read as a league table: the cities use different inspection rules, outcomes and publication systems. NYC's C grade is not equivalent to a Chicago failure, an SF score below 80 or a UK rating below 3.

Chicago: The city where 22% fail.

Chicago's inspection system is binary. You either pass or you don't. No letter grades, no partial credit. And one in five restaurants fails.

Live data from Chicago Open Data · Pass / Fail / Conditional Open full map →

The red dots are outright failures. The yellow dots are conditional passes — restaurants that passed with conditions that must be corrected. Together, 40% of Chicago restaurants have some form of issue on their latest inspection.

San Francisco: Only 41% score above 90.

SF uses a 0–100 scoring system — the most granular of any city. A perfect score is rare. Most restaurants hover between 80 and 95. Below 80 means serious problems.

Live data from SF Open Data · Numeric scores 0–100 Open full map →

London: A high share of top ratings.

70% of Westminster restaurants have the top rating of 5. Only 5% are rated 2 or below. London's system is based on penalty points — and most restaurants keep their score low enough to stay at 5.

Live data from Food Standards Agency · Ratings 0–5 Open full map →

Temperature control is a recurring inspection risk.

Temperature control, handwashing, pest control, clean food-contact surfaces and adequate food-safety procedures recur in official inspection rules and records. But the agencies categorise them differently, and the UK ratings feed does not publish a comparable item-level violation table.

Practical takeaway: a dated temperature check is useful only when it leads to action. Record the reading, the limit used, the corrective action and the person who completed it. Do not use this cross-city snapshot to infer a universal violation ranking.

Cuisine categories show different grade distributions.

In the NYC snapshot, the share of B and C grades varies across the cuisine categories recorded by the city. Those labels are broad and the result can reflect many confounders, including business size, location, inspection frequency and the way cuisines are classified.

This is not evidence that any cuisine is inherently less safe. Operational complexity is one plausible hypothesis, but this descriptive analysis does not test causation. The defensible conclusion is narrower: food businesses should build controls around their actual menu, process and risk profile rather than a category average.

Methodology, sources and limitations.

The numbers in this article are a preserved April 2026 snapshot. The embedded maps query live agency data and may therefore show different totals today.

How the snapshot was assembled

  1. NYC: A, B and C records from NYC Open Data were ordered by inspection date and deduplicated by CAMIS, retaining the most recent graded inspection per establishment.
  2. Chicago: Pass, Fail and Pass with Conditions records were ordered by inspection date and deduplicated by business name plus address.
  3. San Francisco: scored inspections were ordered by inspection date and deduplicated by business name plus address.
  4. London: Westminster establishments were read from the FSA Food Hygiene Rating API. Scotland uses a different rating scheme and is not part of this 0–5 comparison.
  5. Interpretation: the cities' grades are described side by side, not converted into a common score. Percentages are rounded and may not sum to 100.
