Food hygiene certificate Scotland: a 2026 guide to business compliance

Which level your team actually needs, REHIS vs Highfield, real cost, how long a certificate lasts, and the compliance work that starts the day training finishes.

Level 2
Minimum for anyone handling, preparing or serving food
Level 3

Why this isn't optional for a Scottish food business

Food Standards Scotland (FSS) is the regulator, but the actual legal duty sits in the Food Hygiene (Scotland) Regulations 2006: food handlers must be supervised, instructed and trained in food hygiene commensurate with their work activity. There's no single "food hygiene certificate" for a business as a whole — the law is about people, not paperwork. An accredited certificate is simply the standard way a business proves that duty has been met, and it's one of the first things an Environmental Health Officer will ask to see.

Get this wrong and the exposure isn't abstract: undertrained staff are a leading cause of the cross-contamination, temperature and hygiene failures that cap a Food Hygiene Information Scheme (FHIS) rating and turn a routine visit into an enforcement conversation.

Level 2 vs Level 3 — which does your team need

The level required tracks the role, not the job title. Most kitchens need a mix of both.

RoleTypical levelCovers
Frontline handlers — chefs, cooks, kitchen porters, servers plating food, baristas, deli staffLevel 2Personal hygiene, cross-contamination, cleaning, chilling, cooking, storage
Supervisors & managers — head chefs, kitchen managers, business ownersLevel 3Hazard analysis, control measures, running or managing a HACCP system
Support staff — cleaners, maintenance working in food areasAwarenessHygiene zones, cross-contamination basics — full certificate not usually required

Business type shifts the mix too. A coffee shop selling pre-packaged snacks needs far less depth than a full-service kitchen handling raw meat, or a care-home caterer serving a vulnerable population — high-risk operations should push a higher proportion of staff toward Level 3, not just the one named manager.

Step 1 — Choose an accredited provider

Accreditation is the whole game. An unaccredited course — however cheap — won't satisfy the legal duty and won't hold up when an EHO asks who trained your team. Two awarding bodies cover the overwhelming majority of valid certificates in Scotland:

  • REHIS (Royal Environmental Health Institute of Scotland) — the leading Scotland-specific awarding body. Elementary Food Hygiene is widely offered by councils and colleges, independently rated at SCQF level 5, and now available by e-learning (the final exam is still sat at an approved centre).
  • Highfield Qualifications — a UK-wide awarding body whose Level 2 Food Safety for Catering and Level 3 qualifications are just as valid in Scotland as REHIS, and are fully deliverable online, assessment included.

Either route works — pick based on delivery method (online for flexibility, in-person for hands-on demonstration) and provider reviews. Expect to pay roughly £20-£50 for a Level 2 course online, more for Level 3 or in-person delivery. Be sceptical of anything marketed as a completely free accredited certificate — genuine accreditation carries real assessment cost.

Step 2 — Complete the training and pass the assessment

Courses at both levels cover the same core ground: food safety hazards (biological, chemical, physical, allergenic), personal hygiene, safe handling from delivery to service, cleaning and disinfection, pest control, waste management, and allergen control under Natasha's Law. Level 3 adds hazard analysis and HACCP system management.

The final assessment is typically a multiple-choice exam — a Level 2 pass usually needs around 80% correct (e.g. 20 of 25 questions). Most providers allow re-sits, sometimes for a small fee. Understanding the principles gets you through more reliably than memorising answers, and it's the difference that shows up on a busy shift.

Step 3 — Get the certificate, then actually check it

Online courses issue a digital certificate by email almost immediately after a pass; some providers post a physical copy for an extra fee. Before filing it away, verify three things: the name is spelled correctly, the qualification level and awarding body are clearly stated, and the issue date is legible. An EHO will expect to see this during an inspection, so store it somewhere your team can actually retrieve it — not just in an inbox nobody checks.

No certificate carries a printed expiry date — but Food Standards Scotland recommends refreshing knowledge every 3 years, since guidance and best practice move on. Track refresh dates the same way you'd track any other compliance deadline, not as an afterthought.

Step 4 — Make it stick, not just a filing exercise

Certification is the starting point, not the finish line. What actually protects customers and your rating is daily discipline: handwashing and personal hygiene, keeping raw and cooked food apart, accurate temperature control at every stage, a working cleaning schedule, and proactive allergen communication. None of that is optional just because someone passed an exam eighteen months ago.

Underneath the daily practice sits the paperwork an EHO will actually ask for: temperature logs, cleaning sign-offs, delivery checks, incident records, and — the one people lose track of — staff training certificates with their refresh dates. Paper records go missing, fade, or simply never get filed consistently, which is precisely the gap that shows up as a lower Confidence in Management score during an inspection.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a food hygiene certificate cost in Scotland?

A Level 2 / REHIS Elementary certificate typically costs £20-£50 online. Level 3 (supervisors and managers) starts from around £100 and goes up depending on the provider and whether it's delivered online or in person. In-person courses usually cost more once venue and instructor time are factored in.

Can I do a food hygiene certificate online in Scotland?

Yes. REHIS Elementary Food Hygiene is available by e-learning, though candidates still sit the final REHIS exam at an approved training centre. Highfield and other UK-wide awarding bodies deliver fully online Level 2 and Level 3 courses with online assessment. Either route is legally valid in Scotland as long as the provider and qualification are accredited.

Is a food hygiene certificate a legal requirement in Scotland?

Yes, in substance. The Food Hygiene (Scotland) Regulations 2006 require food handlers to be supervised, instructed and trained in food hygiene matters relevant to their work. The law doesn't name one specific certificate, but an accredited Level 2 (food handlers) or Level 3 (supervisors/managers) certificate is the standard way businesses demonstrate they've met that duty during an EHO inspection.

Can I get a free food hygiene certificate in Scotland?

Genuinely free accredited certificates are rare — accredited training involves real assessment and awarding-body costs. Food Standards Scotland does offer free introductory online food hygiene and allergen training, but a formal accredited certificate (REHIS or Highfield) almost always carries a fee, usually £20-£50 at Level 2. Treat "completely free certificate" claims with caution unless the awarding body is named and verifiable.

How long does a food hygiene certificate last in Scotland?

Certificates don't carry a hard legal expiry date printed on them. Food Standards Scotland recommends refreshing food hygiene knowledge every 3 years, since practices and guidance change. Many businesses treat 3 years as the working renewal cycle and log it alongside other staff training records.
